01 · Physics

The Universe of Forces

Tap to relaunch

Every step, the planet is pulled toward the star by Newton's law of gravitation, a = GM/r². Launch it at exactly the circular speed √(GM/r) and it circles; slower, and it falls into an ellipse; at √2 times that speed its energy reaches zero and it escapes for good. For every closed orbit, the period squared grows with the orbit's size cubed: Kepler's third law. The dipping grid is only a picture of the pull.

02 · Chemistry

The Molecular World

Drag to turn

These are the real shapes of the hydrogen atom's electron. Each dot is one place the electron could be found, drawn with probability |ψ|² from the exact wavefunction; gold and periwinkle show where ψ is positive or negative. Where the colours meet, ψ is zero: a node, where the electron is never found.

03 · Mathematics

The Infinite Dimension

Click to zoom in ×4

Take a number c, start at z = 0 and repeat z → z² + c. If z stays small forever, c belongs to the Mandelbrot set (the dark shape); the colours show how quickly every other point escapes. Its edge never runs out of detail: click anywhere to zoom in, and familiar shapes keep returning at every scale.

04 · Geometry

The Architecture of Reality

Drag to turn

There are exactly five convex solids whose faces are identical regular polygons, known since ancient Greece. For every one of them, vertices − edges + faces = 2: Euler's formula. The tesseract is the cube's four-dimensional cousin. It turns through a fourth axis and we see its shadow in 3D, which is why the inner cube seems to turn inside out.

05 · Biology

The Living Universe

DNA's two strands wind around each other about once every 10.5 base pairs, or 3.4 nm. A always pairs with T through two hydrogen bonds, and G with C through three, so each strand holds the full instructions for the other. Unzip it to watch the strands part as they do at a replication fork, where each becomes the template for a new partner.

06 · Quantum

Beyond Reality

Particles leave the source one at a time, yet together they build bands, an interference pattern, as if each passed through both slits as a wave. The bands are spaced Δy = λL/d (here the screen is 1 m away). Switch on a detector to learn which slit each particle used, and the bands vanish. The ripples drawn here picture the probability wave; they are not literal water waves.

07 · Astronomy & Cosmology

Beyond the Stars

A star's colour is a thermometer. Every hot body glows with a spectrum set only by its temperature (Planck's law), and the peak of that glow moves to shorter waves as it heats up: λ = b/T, Wien's law. Cool stars like Betelgeuse glow orange-red, the Sun peaks in green-blue yet looks white, and hot stars like Rigel burn blue-white, putting most of their light into the ultraviolet. Astronomers sort stars into classes O, B, A, F, G, K and M by exactly this.

08 · Computer Science & AI

The Living Digital World

Every algorithm is a recipe a computer follows exactly. Here four real recipes sort the same kind of shuffled list, and you can watch every comparison (the bright bars) as it happens. Bubble and insertion sort compare almost every pair, so their work grows with the square of the list, O(n²); merge sort and quicksort split the problem in halves and need only about n log n steps. For a million items, that is the difference between a trillion steps and twenty million.

09 · Earth & Environment

Our Climate in One Equation

Earth warms until the heat it gives off balances the sunlight it absorbs: T = [S(1 − α) / 4εσ]^¼. With no greenhouse effect the planet would average about −18 °C; greenhouse gases send part of the outgoing heat back down and lift it to about 15 °C. Brighter ice reflects more sunlight, which is why melting ice speeds warming. This one-number model leaves out oceans, clouds and feedbacks, but it gets Earth's temperature right from first principles.

10 · Engineering & Technology

The Physics of a Smooth Ride

A car's suspension is a spring that stores the bump's energy and a damper that turns it into heat: m x″ + c x′ + k x = 0. One number, the damping ratio ζ, decides the ride. Below 1 the car bounces; above 1 it creeps back slowly and feels harsh; at exactly 1 (critical damping) it settles fastest without a single bounce. Engineers tune real cars a little below 1, trading a soft first bounce for comfort.
